"What is Backstreets?"

Introduction

Final Project Introduction
“Backstreets” by Bruce Springsteen takes an outsider’s position on many of the themes discussed both within rock music and the Born to Run album itself. It provides commentary on patterns of nostalgia and regret, relating them both to the audience and the Born to Run album. Nostalgia provides the primary framework for this project, and the different lenses through which the song’s nostalgia can be interpreted are displayed within it. “Backstreets” simultaneously achieves three primary objectives: it criticizes the patterns of indulgence that the album’s earlier narrators rely on, warns the listener against delaying the confrontation of interpersonal conflicts, and tells an emotionally charged story of an interpersonal relationship gone awry. “Backstreets” projects its argumentation through evocative instrumentation and lyrics that follow a clear narrative, ultimately creating a song that starkly contrasts with Born to Run’s opening half. This project is centered around the usage of nostalgia within “Backstreets” and its various applications to the audience, Born to Run, and rock music as a whole, ultimately outlining how “Backstreets” creates a relationship with the listener that deviates from most rock music of the 1970s, the narrations of Born to Run’s first half, and the expectations of its listeners.
The first centerpiece of my final project focuses on the unique role of nostalgia within Springsteen’s music. In my Critical Reading Assignment, I analyzed Elizabeth M. Seymour’s argument that the nostalgia that characterizes much of Springsteen’s music is representative of the collective nature of the working class. She exemplifies how themes of longing for a distant, more pleasant past characterizes much of the artistic media consumed by American working-class demographics (Seymour 2012, 68). Seymour concludes that the appeal of Springsteen’s music to these demographics stems largely from its unique ability to capture their experiences and ideals in a highly authentic manner, pointing to Springsteen’s working-class upbringing and highly personal narratives as reasoning. In complement to Seymour’s piece, Hannah Aredent’s philosophical work The Human Condition characterizes the processes of creation and destruction as the essence of working-class lives (Ardent 1958). “Backstreets,” which explores the two within the context of personal relationships, both embodies and elaborates on Ardent’s theories. In part, this project seeks to exemplify the role that Springsteen’s music as a whole plays in representing the nature of the working class, with nostalgia being the medium of choice through which Springsteen represents these characteristics.
The first subset of the project’s nostalgia section discusses the sense of personal alignment that Springsteen creates between himself and the audience. This personalization is represented through both the characteristics of Springsteen’s narratives on the album and the means by which Springsteen has represented the album decades after its release. As outlined in my Music Review Essay, the narrative of “Backstreets” serves as a direct interaction between Springsteen and the listener; through the story of his relationship with Terry, Springsteen warns the listener against evading confrontation and indulging in temporary means of gratification. In addition to narratives that directly address the listener, Springsteen further creates the sense of Born to Run being a shared experience through later representations of the album, namely Wings for Wheels, a documentary that captures the entire process of writing and recording the album. The documentary depicts Springsteen going through auditory and visual archives from the album’s creation, discussing personal details about his life at the time, and driving through his hometown with the viewer’s perspective positioned in the passenger seat (Zimny 2005). The documentary visually presents Springsteen as an equal to the viewer, providing a sense of companionship and relatability to his narratives and their messages. Similarly, in the Playlist and Listening guide essay, I discuss the various uses of nostalgia within a broad variety of music, as well as Springsteen’s specific applications of nostalgia as a means of creating appeal to his audience. The flexible usage of nostalgia within this essay presents an exploration into the role of nostalgia within the lives of each song’s narrators and audiences; this essay engages with multiple interpretations of nostalgia with the objective of further exemplifying its emotional strength within musical narratives. The second subset of the project’s nostalgia section explores how “Backstreets” presents a criticism of many of the typical narratives of rock music, a concept I hope to explore in greater depth in the final essay through the lenses of race and expressions of sexuality through rock music.
The second centerpiece of the final project’s layout asks the viewer a seemingly insurmountable question: “What is ‘Backstreets?’” This section explores the role of critical elements that are fully unique to the song, namely its instrumentation and themes of sexuality, with the objective of defining the song’s significance for a listener less familiarized with Springsteen’s music. For example, the instrumentation section discusses Willaim Ruhlmann’s explanation of Springsteen’s usage of the Hammond Organ as a callback to Bob Dylan’s “Like a Rolling Stone,” solidifying the themes that the song primarily seeks to convey, namely bitter nostalgia and regret (Ruhlmann 2025). Additionally, through revisiting my Music Review Essay’s further elaboration on the song’s instrumental structure, the instrumentation section demonstrates how various non-lyrical elements play a crucial role in facilitating the song’s narrative. This section also outlines the role of sexuality within “Backstreets,” engaging with numerous critical analyses of Terry’s role within both the narrator and Springsteen’s life (Hiatt et al. 2018). Terry, whom critics have interpreted to be a homosexual lover, a friend, or a real person within Springsteen’s life, induces a sense of ambiguity that broadens the ability of “Backstreets” to appeal to a diverse audience. These two elements ultimately combine to form the core of the song’s narrative and set the stage for a broader discussion on the song’s role within the context of rock music’s history and the Born to Run album.
