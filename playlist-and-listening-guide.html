Playlist & Listening Guide

Nostalgia

Playlist Listening Guide Essay

Playlist:

  1. Khruangbin. “Connaissais de Face” from Mordechai. Dead Oceans Inc.: 2020.
  2. Harry McClintock. “The Big Rock Candy Mountain” from Bona Fide Bluegrass and Mountain Music. BMG Heritage: 2002. 
  3. AL-90, Spurv. “Melancholia Staroy Pornozvezdy” from Code-915913. Fuselab: 2015.
  4. Simon and Garfunkel. “America” from Bookends. Columbia Records: 1968.
  5. The Smiths. “Back to the Old House - 2011 Remaster” from Louder Than Bombs. Warner Music UK: 1987.
  6. Bruce Springsteen. “The River” from The River. Columbia Records: 1980. 
  7. Bruce Springsteen. “Backstreets” from Born to Run. Columbia Records: 1975. 
  8. Floating Points, Pharoah Sanders, London Symphony Orchestra. “Movement 6” from Promises. Luaka Bop, Inc.: 2021.

“Is a dream a lie if it don’t come true, or is it something worse?” 

-Bruce Springsteen, “The River”

A dusty photograph from a simpler time. A late-night conversation with an old friend. A vivid, infuriatingly brief glimpse of the past provided by an old tape recording that lingers for one surreal moment before the memory once again becomes intangible. You’re reminded of the smell of thawing spring soil or that of a lovingly prepared meal, the presence of a loved one or the grief of losing them, the things that you took for granted or the things you’re astonished to have persevered through. Try as you may to accurately recreate the feeling of being so deeply intertwined with your past, the absolute best you can do is explore the feeling through the stories music tells you. Each reminder transports you to a memory nestled deep within your mind, and their potency leaves you all the more eager to delve into your past experiences. The presence of these memories tends to be fleeting, yet musicians across all time periods, genres, and personal backgrounds try their best to capture them through a wide variety of means. 

In this playlist, personal and interpersonal narratives, interactions between various instrumental elements, and emulating technology of the past are all used to express and explore concepts of nostalgia. Through these vastly different approaches, each artist featured in this playlist strives towards the creation of art that explores the past memories of themselves and the listener, as well as the general feeling of interacting with such memories. This playlist adopts a flexible interpretation of nostalgia, exploring how different musical narrators discuss fantastical places with such a mental fixation that they become as embedded within their lives as real ones. This definition of nostalgia gives way to an exploration of how dwelling intensely on such memories often creates complications within the listener’s present life. The playlist examines grief and its relation to nostalgia, demonstrating how one’s interaction with a bitterly nostalgic past gives way to processes of grieving. 

The playlist opens with “Connaissais de Face” by Khruangbin, a trio from Houston known for its engagement with a wide variety of global musical influences. The song is an auditory portrait of a sentimental conversation between two characters, possibly old friends, who reflect on their shared experiences and the people that accompanied them throughout. The title roughly translates to “recognition of the face” or “knowing someone by face,” creating the image of someone that one never truly meets but “knows” to a certain extent anyway. The song evokes a sense of tender nostalgia, further aided by spacious vocals and shimmering guitar and keyboard  licks that seem to mimic an affectionate dialogue. As a whole, the characters in “Connaissais de Face” explore a past that they are physically removed from yet still connected to through their relationship with each other. This song presents a highly conventional take on nostalgia, with classical representations of untroubled and less abstract memories. It serves as an introduction to nostalgia, setting up the listener for a deeper dissection of nostalgia within the next few songs. 

The second song on the playlist is “The Big Rock Candy Mountain” by Harry McClintock, an American folk singer whose music was influenced by decades of working on the railroads and hoboing throughout the 1890s and early 1900s. Originally performed by McClintock as a busking piece and recorded in 1928, the song reflects a certain brand of fantasticism that is characteristic of many Depression-era American folk songs. The narrator, presumably a railroad worker, sings of their vision of a fantastical world in which their struggles are wholly erased and “handouts grow on bushes,” “all the cops have wooden legs,” and “there’s a lake of stew, and of whiskey, too.” The realism with which the narrator describes this place exudes a sense of nostalgia for it, as if finding the Big Rock Candy Mountains means returning to a romanticized figment of their imagination or a simpler time in the narrator’s memory. 

The third song on the playlist is “Melancholia Staroy Pornozvezdy” by AL-90, a producer with a fairly limited public presence from Murmansk, Russia (a city that lies in a remote region 125 miles above the Arctic Circle). The song, whose title roughly translates to “Melancholy of an Old Pornstar,” introduces a certain sense of dreary nostalgia through the usage of specific productional elements rather than a vocal narrative. The song most prominently loops sampled vocals and a violin, all played through a tape machine or a similar audio effect, creating the vision of a distant memory captured in a dusty photograph or an old cassette. “Melancholia Staroy Poronozvezdy” presents a more abstract embodiment of nostalgia, in which an old story is told through the image it seeks to render in the listener’s mind. It also represents a balance between the blissful detachment of “Connaissais de Face” and “The Big Rock Candy Mountain” and the brutal realization of “Back to the Old House” and “Backstreets.” 

The strain of fantastical nostalgia depicted in “The Big Rock Candy Mountain” is fully deconstructed in Simon and Garfunkel’s “America,” a narrative that follows the singer and a companion named Kathy as they make a journey from Saginaw, Michigan “to look for America.” Their journey begins with optimism and excitement, with the characters cracking jokes and finding themselves fully comfortable with unpleasant conditions on their journey. As their journey continues, the romanticism of their vision of “America” becomes evident; the final verse closes with the lines: “I’m empty and aching and I don’t know why / Counting the cars on the New Jersey Turnpike / They’ve all come to look for America.” As the song closes, “America” is realized to be a universally romanticized place, as each character in the song has their own fantastical imagination of such a place. The collective vision of that place, in which objects of fantasy are conflated with each character’s actual surroundings, is ultimately dismantled.

“Back to the Old House” by The Smiths introduces a new concept within the exploration of nostalgia, namely the conflict of bad memories and a desire to reconcile with them or even experience them again. The song expands the listener’s perception of nostalgia from beyond the fantastical memories depicted in “America” to decidedly painful ones, demonstrating how nostalgia can seep into even the most seemingly inaccessible facets of one’s mind. The narrative revolves around the “old house,” possibly the residence of a past lover. The song opens with the repetition of the lines “I would rather not go back to the old house / There’s too many bad memories,” establishing the narrator’s desire to remain separate from the experiences they attach to that place. Despite their stoicism, as the song progresses, the narrator seems to develop a sense of curiosity surrounding the house: “And you never knew / How much I really liked you… / Are you still there?” By continuing to return to the same place in their mind, the narrator ultimately reopens an old wound, ruminating about the house until they give into their curiosity: “I would love to go back to the old house / But I never will.” The reemergence of such memories and their subsequent bitter nostalgia sets the tone for the rest of the playlist, in which each song’s respective narrations and instrumentation paint a different picture of an ever-painful past. 

Bruce Springsteen’s “Backstreets” lives within the narrator’s nostalgia, as they reflect on a past relationship and its unforeseen deterioration. The song opens as a recollection of the narrator’s relationship with Terry, foreshadowing their fallout yet remaining collected until the bridge, at which point the song opens into an emotionally charged burst of energy that is characterized by Spingsteen’s harsh vocals and the song’s forcefully presented instrumentation. The song follows the course of an emotional outburst by the narrator, in which they tell a story, lose control of their temper, and reign themselves in as the song closes. Similarly to “Back to the Old House,” “Backstreets” begins as a benign recollection that consumes the narrator throughout the course of the song. The nostalgia that “Backstreets” initially explores ultimately becomes a present experience; through the dramatic evolution of the song, Springsteen demonstrates the capabilities of bitter memories to reemerge in one’s present life. 

“The River,” also by Bruce Springsteen, transitions the concepts of bitter nostalgia into a more philosophical and removed sense. The song follows the narrator’s recollection of his transition into manhood alongside his high school girlfriend, Mary. The river is recalled by the narrator as a place of escape, primarily from the realities of an entrance into adult life characterized by personal and financial instability. The narrator of the song reflects on such sentimental past experiences with a sense of fear: “At night on them banks I’d lie awake / And pull her close just to feel each breath she’d take / Now those memories come back to haunt me / They haunt me like a curse.” Rather than simply presenting a vision of bitter nostalgia, “The River” expands on such memories to include the narrator openly discussing their relationship with them. By outwardly examining the role of bitter nostalgia within their mind, the narrator ultimately projects grief for the long-lost sense of escape that the river now represents: “...that sends me down to the river / though I’d know the river is dry.” The closing lines of “The River” invite the listener to explore the grief connected to their own memories, ultimately personalizing the experience of the playlist’s closing track. 

The playlist closes with “Movement 6” by Floating Points, Pharoah Sanders, and the London Symphony Orchestra. This song, the sixth movement of a nine-part album, seems to evoke a portrait of a single memory and the evolution of grief surrounding it as the listener continues to dwell on its existence. The song opens with, and ultimately revolves around, a softly repeated series of seven notes. This leitmotif is repeated with slight variations behind a light veil of strings that grows in rigidity with each repetition. The surrounding instruments grow in conflict and weight as the listener grows increasingly preoccupied with the seven-note memory; the strength of the leitmotif increases as well, as if its constant repetition formulates an increased presence in this listener’s mind. Finally, as the memory recedes into the depths of the listener’s mind in the final thirty seconds, it brings with itself a piece of the veil of strings, as if the memory of grief itself has become a facet of the memory. The song creates a musical representation of a distant memory resurfacing and reattaching emotional significance to the listener, ultimately formulating a process of grief that expands and smooths itself out over the course of the song. 

This playlist serves as a portrait for embodiments of nostalgia in the listener’s mind. While some memories are regained through interpersonal relationships, as in the case of Khruangbin’s “Connaissais de Face,” others reemerge from interaction with relics of the past, a concept that is abstractly demonstrated in AL-90’s “Melancholia Staroy Pornozvezdy.” “The Big Rock Candy Mountain” by Harry McClintock and “America” by Simon and Garfunkel both explore a fantasy that is so deeply intertwined with the narrator’s lives that they reflect on it as if it were one part of their lived experience. The next three songs, “Back to the Old House” by the Smiths and “Backstreets” and “The River” by Bruce Springsteen explore different means of grappling with painful nostalgia. Their respective narrators open old wounds, find themselves overwhelmed when exploring their past experiences, and live in fear of the memories associated with a more optimistic past. The journey of a bitter memory is ultimately documented in Floating Points’s “Movement 6,” in which each repetition of the memory increases the sense of conflict within the listener’s mind. When taken as a whole, the playlist charts the simultaneous journeys of multiple different embodiments of nostalgia and insights into how humans are conditioned to deal with them. The playlist reminds the listener of the relentless presence of nostalgia and the diverse ways in which it influences them. By presenting many different embodiments of nostalgia on one playlist, this playlist serves as an all-encompassing atlas of our interactions with nostalgia.
