Review of “Backstreets” by Bruce Springsteen

Bruce Springsteen’s “Backstreets” immerses the listener within the intense emotional journey of its narrator and the downfall of their relationship with a character named Terry. The song synthesizes evocative instrumentation and narrative lyrics to depict the sense of bitter nostalgia and convoluted emotions embedded within such narratives. Released in 1975 through Columbia Records, “Backstreets” concludes the first side of Springsteen’s Born to Run album and represents a strong shift in the album’s narrative qualities. The first half of Born to Run portrays characters that are determined to ignore the harsh nature of their lives; tracks such as “Thunder Road,” “Tenth Avenue Freeze-Out,” and “Night” depict cars and sexual relationships as reliable means of escape that the narrators unwaveringly turn to. These songs use more cinematic instrumentation and hopeful lyrics to depict the narrators’ sense of trust in these means, though they still hint at a sense of instability that the narrators willfully ignore. The second half of the album, however, succumbs to the lingering sense of despair depicted in the first half, a facet of Born to Run’s narrative that “Backstreets” helps realize. Through a narrative with a more pessimistic voice, “Backstreets” directly provides a criticism of the patterns of temporary escape that the narrators in the first half of Born to Run reliably indulge themselves in. Through “Backstreets,” Springsteen invites the listener to consider the harmful means of mental escape that they engage with and warns them of becoming too reliant on such means. 

Each instrumental element in “Backstreets” plays a crucial role in supporting the emotional qualities of Springsteen’s narration. The song opens with a forcefully presented piano melody which urgently breaks ahead of the backing instruments and reins itself back in with every measure. The piano is backed by a bass guitar that trembles on one note. It sustains the sense of unease induced by the piano and eventually aligns itself with the piano as the two transition into the rest of the instrumental introduction. The introduction is characterized by the sudden presence of a vibrant Hammond organ, solidifying the wall of sound that is rapidly built within the opening minute of the song. Numerous critics regard the melody and tone of the Hammond organ to be highly reminiscent of Bob Dylan’s “Like a Rolling Stone,” a song that explores many similar themes to “Backstreets,” including hopelessness and bitter nostalgia. The Hammond organ’s unique resonance and vibrato capabilities make it something of an otherworldly relative to a human voice and a highly emotive musical tool (Ruhlmann 2025). With the introduction of resonant drums that stumble in the wake of the piano and a sentimental call-and-response between the Hammond organ and an electric guitar, the instrumental then opens itself into an auditory stage for Springsteen’s lyrics. Ultimately, the instrumental serves as a musical map of the narrator’s life for the listener, as it helps guide their understanding of the song’s narrative.

The narrative of “Backstreets” recounts the narrator’s relationship with a gender-ambiguous character named Terry, a name that appears in the lyrics of several other Bruce Springsteen tracks. Terry’s role within the narrator’s life is left largely up to interpretation, and critics have analyzed them to be a covert homosexual lover, a pseudonym for one of Springsteen’s ex-girlfriends, or a platonic friend (Hiatt et al. 2018). Regardless of Terry’s literal role, each reading of the song creates a similar narrative of companionship turned sour. The opening lines of the song set the tone for the evolution of the narrator’s relationship with Terry: “One soft-infested summer, me and Terry became friends / Trying in vain to breathe the fire we were born in.” The description of breathing fire as the narrator and Terry’s interaction with their natural environment creates a sense of delayed but inevitable conflict within their relationship. This conveyed desperation further materializes within the later lyrics, which repeatedly bring up the concept of running from something intangible, usually some embodiment of an inevitable rift. The realization of such inevitability unfolds throughout the proceeding verses, in which the disregard of their relationship’s volatile nature comes to a collapse: “At night sometimes it seemed you could hear the whole damn city crying / Blame it on the lies that killed us, blame it on the truth that ran us down / You can blame it all on me, Terry, it don't matter to me now.” Though the lies that the narrator and Terry indulge themselves in are never exactly specified, the more prominent concept that the lyrics explore is that of avoidance; when avoidance becomes impossible, the characters find themselves forced to confront the very existence of their relationship. “Backstreets” depicts alcohol (“...tying faith between our teeth / Sleeping in that old abandoned beach house, getting wasted in the heat”) and possibly sexual relationships (“...laying here in the dark, you're like an angel on my chest / Just another tramp of hearts crying tears of faithlessness”) as means of escape, comparable to those that are discussed earlier in the Born to Run album. The difference, however, is that “Backstreets” introduces the concept of personal collapse as a product of reliance on such means, presenting a darker deviation from the brutal optimism of Born to Run’s first half. Through the story of the narrator and their relationship with Terry, Springsteen presents a commentary on how delaying the confrontation of one’s interpersonal conflicts makes such relationships prone to collapse. “Backstreets” ultimately warns the listener that their relationships are much less stable than they may expect. 

Every individual element of “Backstreets” serves the purpose of creating a highly emotive rebuttal to the hopeful nature of Born to Run’s first half. Each piece of the instrumental initially evokes a sense of urgency that ultimately gives in to despair as the plot of the narrator and Terry’s relationship takes a dark turn. By bringing every element of “Backstreets” to align itself behind the same narrative, Springsteen created both a song that continues to resonate with young demographics nearly half a century after its release and a brutal commentary on the patterns of self-destructive human behavior with respect to their personal relationships.
