"What is Backstreets?"

Final Essay

Music Review

Nostalgia

Spectacle for Some, Familiarity for Others: Born to Run, the Hammond Organ, and Exoticism in 20th-century Rock Music

Released in 1975 as his first widely acclaimed album, Bruce Springsteen’s album Born to Run stood out for its synthesis of distinctively dramatic instrumentation and highly narrative storylines. The specific elements of the album’s innovative character are revealed through both its interactions with Springsteen’s musical influences and the listener themselves. “Backstreets,” the fourth song on the album, represents a turning point in both the album’s narratives and instrumentation. One of the most distinctive features of the song is its use of the Hammond organ, an instrument that was implemented in a wide variety of music throughout the decades leading up to Born to Run’s release. First manufactured in 1935 and popularized throughout the 1940s and 1950s, the Hammond organ served as an instrumental staple of multiple genres of music by the time “Backstreets” was written. The hammond organ had come to define the sound of African-American jazz and gospel music as early as 1939 and remained a staple of such music through the 1970s (Crawley 2016, 9).

For the first several decades of its prominence, the Hammond organ was predominantly a characteristic of the era’s African-American music, playing a prominent role in both mainstream and localized African-American music. Jazz historian David H. Rosenthal’s article “Jazz in the Ghetto: 1950-70” exemplifies how the Hammond organ permeated many popular jazz releases, as a preference held by record labels for “listenable” jazz music placed numerous organists at the forefront of the genre’s popularity (Rosenthal 1988, 52).  Similarly, the introduction of the Hammond organ to African-American churches beginning in the 1940s made it a staple of gospel music. In “That There Might Be Black Thought: Nothing Music and the Hammond B-3,” a self-proclaimed love letter to the Hammond organ, Ashon Crawley demonstrates how the organ reflected black religious and intellectual ideals through instrumental expression, adding an additional dimension to the community’s sermons and lyrics. Additionally, serving as a more affordable and durable substitute for the pipe organs that traditionally permeated white and upper-class churches, the Hammond organ was originally popularized for gospel music in inner-city storefront churches in Chicago (Crawley 2016, 21). These two incentives for the Hammond organ’s particularly widespread use in black communities and music made it representative of an increasingly distinct African-American sound.

Though “Backstreets” was hardly the first piece of rock music released by a white artist to utilize the Hammond organ, its usage in tandem with the song’s themes of nostalgia offered a presentation of the instrument that deviated from the typical patterns of its usage at the time. As explained by Elizabeth Seymour in her paper “‘Where Dreams are Found and Lost’: Springsteen, Nostalgia, and Identity,” Springsteen’s music has historically resonated with a predominantly white, working-class audience (Seymour 2012, 61). With the Hammond organ serving as a distinct element of African-American music, its usage signals an attempt to align the song’s narrative with a broader audience. Through its highly personal nature, Bruce Springsteen draws a personal connection between himself, the listener, and the song’s musical influences through the employment of the Hammond organ on “Backstreets.” Drawing on the Hammond organ’s frequently religious nature, Springsteens induces a sense of cultural familiarity within the listener and lends a greater sense of legitimacy to the narrator’s message. Ultimately, the Hammond organ’s presence in “Backstreets” offers a unique interpretation of its importance, in which the song seeks to align itself with a broader audience rather than simply commodifying the audience’s identity. 

The marketing and popularization of characteristically “black” music throughout the 1950s and 1960s helped facilitate the Hammond organ’s effectiveness in “Backstreets.” The Hammond organ’s rise to prominence occurred in tandem with the popularization of early rock music. The genre’s marketing as “black” music made it more sensational for white audiences, accelerating rock music’s rise. As white artists such as Bruce Springsteen and Bob Dylan experimented with the Hammond organ throughout the 1960s and 1970s, they were inherently borrowing from African-American creative influences and implementing them in their own music. The embrace of these influences was part of a greater process of creative exchange between black and white artists, though such a process was encouraged by black artists’ music being marketed as an exotic, otherworldly commodity (Redd 1985, 4). Such an exchange rendered the Hammond organ as a bridge between two distinct musical cultures. To Springsteen, this connection served as a means of expressing familiarity to some listeners while maintaining an otherworldly appeal to others. This contrast made “Backstreets” both a personally engaging and exotic piece of music to a broad target audience. Furthermore, English professor Robert Springer highlights the systematic manner with which record companies exploited black creative works: "The socio-economic position of African-Americans in the South, their frequent illiteracy and the fact that they were novices in the world of commercial music, made them easy prey who could be exploited without contract or persuaded to sign away their rights" (Springer 2007, 39). Ultimately, what was sometimes referred to as a "cultural exchange" was more of a one-sided transaction.

In addition to the sense of exoticism expressed in earlier rock pieces, the use of the Hammond organ in “Backstreets” seeks to create a personalized relationship between the narrator and the listener. Well beyond its instrumentation, “Backstreets” revolves around directly addressing the listener and inviting them to personally consider the implications of the song’s narration, a tactic that is upheld by the presence of the Hammond organ. While it is unlikely that the Hammond organ’s presence in the song was a direct or successful attempt to capture the attention of African-American listeners, the instrument’s role as an African-American cultural implement inherently makes it an instrumental tool to engage with a more diverse set of listeners and complement the song’s narrative. The Hammond organ’s presence in “Backstreets” lends the song a sense of familiarity, thereby making its narrative more intimate and the listener personally involved in the lyrics. With the Hammond organ representing a distinctly African-American musical style, the instrumentation of “Backstreets” engages with a wider variety of musical influences and incorporates musical elements familiar with a broader audience. This stylistic and cultural breadth creates a sense of proximity between the song’s narrative and the listener’s life. 

Initially, the Hammond organ most prominently permeated inner-city churches and African-American music. Its use in such music rendered it an emblem of African-American music, and its popularization amongst white audiences and musicians in the 1960s was directly linked with its appeal as an exotically “black” instrument. In general, the instrument’s usage in the music of white rock musicians served as an attempt to engage with the exotic appeal of the genre’s roots in African-American music. However, because of its personalized familiarity, “Backstreets” also presents another unique use of the Hammond organ, serving the direct purpose of upholding the narrator’s warning: avoidance through indulgence is a thoroughly dangerous tactic. “Backstreets” lies within a larger process of African-American culture being appropriated and marketed to white audiences throughout the mid-20th century. Even so, the appeal of its use of the Hammond organ relies less on spectacle and more on the dramatic narration that its instrumental upholds.
