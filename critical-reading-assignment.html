"What is Backstreets?"

Critical Reading Assignment

Nostalgia

Critical Reading Assignment

Chapter 4 of Bruce Springsteen, Cultural Studies, and the Runaway American Dream is a paper titled “‘Where Dreams are Found and Lost’: Springsteen, Nostalgia, and Identity” by Elizabeth M. Seymour, a professor of anthropology and history at Penn State University. Seymour analyzes Bruce Springsteen’s continuous usage of working-class characters in the stories his music presents and its relevance to his musical audience and their historical experiences throughout Springsteen’s career. Seymour argues that Springsteen’s music has historically strived to authentically capture the emotions and ideals of the working class, playing on popular narratives of both hope and disillusionment as they relate to the American Dream (Seymour 2012, 61).

One important element of Springsteen’s life that Seymour discusses is his personal history growing up in a working-class social environment and his current role as a “hero of the working class.” She describes how Springsteen’s background relates to his frequent explorations of the American dream within his music: the promise that hard work creates a more comfortable personal future. Such a notion, Seymour argues, is easily projected by a wide variety of voices and manipulated to serve various ideologies, lending significance to Springsteen’s decisively vocal role in capturing the experiences of the working class. By providing a brief history of the economic turmoil of the 1960s and 1970s, she further contextualizes the importance of his music to its audience and its roots in Springsteen’s lived experiences (61).

The paper begins with the history of the notions of nostalgia and melancholia, which are particularly critical elements of Springsteen’s “Backstreets” and Born to Run as a whole. Analyzing Susan Stewart’s On Longing, Seymour connects Stewart’s exploration of societal nostalgia to its role in Springsteen’s music and its appeal to its target audiences (62). She also engages with William Cunningham Bisell’s paper “Engaging Colonial Nostalgia” to claim that nostalgia itself is responsible for many different Western modes of thought and is a significant facet in the politics and human nature of Western capitalism, ultimately positioning herself to assert the intrinsic appeal of Springsteen’s narratives to its target audiences. She demonstrates nostalgia to be rooted in continuous patterns of socioeconomic change spurred by capitalism, making the American working class to be particularly prone to its appeal. The constant presence of nostalgia in Springsteen’s music, then, is an embodiment of the artist engaging with his working-class roots and appealing to the audience with which he feels most personally aligned (63).

Following a history of the themes present within Born to Run is a history of such themes being applied to Springsteen's music itself, beginning with his first two releases, the less-known Greetings From Asbury Park and The Wild, The Innocent, & the E Street Shuffle. Comparing Born to Run with these two albums, Seymour demonstrates how Born to Run presents a more complicated nostalgia within a narrator’s life. Whereas the narrators in much of Springsteen’s first two albums viewed their past through rose-colored glasses, songs such as “Backstreets” present a decisive departure from such rosiness, dealing with themes of violence, heartbreak, and loneliness. Stewart discusses the role of cars and romantic and sexual relationships as a means of escape from the dark realities of the narrators’ lives, though she ultimately demonstrates that the employment of such means are more of a temporary act of desperation than a meaningful resolution to their personal issues. The article continues to list different “phases” of nostalgia being embodied in Springsteen’s music, including “deep despair,” “romantic love,” and “ambivalent darkness.” When pieced together, each phase represents a single step in the history of how Springsteen’s music plays on the characteristically nostalgic nature of the working class (68). 

In general, Seymour’s interpretation of Springsteen’s music and its relationship with the audience is a serviceable analysis that synthesizes cultural history and philosophy to contextualize his music. The article engages with a variety of sources to present such a portrait of Springsteen, including nonacademic writings from authors with personal connections to him and lyrics from throughout his musical career (64-75). However, the article ultimately reads more like a literary analysis than an artistic one; Seymour’s paper is overly reliant on Springsteen’s verbal narratives to convey her point. While exploring how instrumentation evokes senses of both romantic and bitter nostalgia would require a more abstract mode of thinking, the production of Born to Run is highly evocative and intentionally crafted, no doubt supporting the narratives projected by Springsteen. Seymour’s argument would be enhanced by, for example, the consideration of the dramatic shifts in energy, the shifts between harsh and soothing in Springsteen’s voice, and eccentric guitars that permeate much of Springsteen’s music. The consideration of such musical elements would add another dimension to Seymour’s argument and further provide credibility to her exploration of nostalgia, elements which could be expanded on in personally conducted research. In drawing conclusions on what is ultimately an artistic act, the objective qualities of Springsteen’s music must be considered in their relation to the subjective ones. Additionally, though the article provides some personal context about Springsteen’s upbringing in the working class, it would benefit from some further exploration of how his music reflects actual personal experiences, thereby explaining how his music is partially a projection of personal nostalgia rather than exclusively a collective nostalgia of the working class. 

Seymour’s essay provides a look into the factors that make Bruce Springsteen’s music so effective at resonating with working-class audiences. By engaging themes of both sweet and bitter nostalgia, she argues, Springsteen appeals to the most prominent emotions of such demographics. She draws on philosophical and historical interpretations of nostalgia and Springsteen’s lyrics to demonstrate where his music fits within the social fabric of the working class; the contrast of hope and disillusionment permeates his music, rendering him an artist whose work authentically represents their ethos. Despite its somewhat restrictive focus on elements of storytelling, the paper serves as an excellent introduction to the qualities of Springsteen’s narratives and their relation to the audience.


Works Cited

Seymour, Elizabeth. 2012. “‘Where Dreams are Found and Lost’: Springsteen, Nostalgia, and Identity.” In Bruce Springsteen, Cultural Studies, and the Runaway American Dream, edited by Kenneth Womack, Jerry Zolten, and Mark Bernhard. Ashgate Publishing Limited.
